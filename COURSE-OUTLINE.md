# AI Foundations: A Field Guide for Beginners

A six-module introduction to artificial intelligence for people with little or no technical background.

## Course objective

Understand how AI works, recognize its everyday uses, and build the confidence to use it thoughtfully and responsibly.

## Target audience and prerequisites

For curious beginners, students, and working professionals. If you can browse the web, you have everything you need. No coding, advanced math, or AI experience required.

The exercises include paper-based alternatives. Learners do not need an AI account, a paid subscription, or programming software.

## Duration and format

Six hours of self-paced learning, including practice, over three suggested weeks. Time estimates are guides, not deadlines. Each module combines a short reading, a real-world case, a field exercise, a reflection, and a knowledge check with feedback.

| Week | Modules   | Suggested time | Focus                                             |
| ---- | --------- | -------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| 1    | 01 and 02 | 105 minutes    | AI fundamentals and learning from examples        |
| 2    | 03 and 04 | 135 minutes    | Generative AI, prompting, and useful applications |
| 3    | 05 and 06 | 120 minutes    | Responsible use and a small project               |

## Content themes

- AI fundamentals: The big ideas behind AI, without the technical jargon.
- Data & learning: Explore the relationship between data, patterns, and predictions.
- Tools & prompting: Meet creative AI tools and learn the art of a useful prompt.
- Real-world applications: Find practical uses in business, creativity, and daily life.
- Risks & ethics: Navigate bias, privacy, and the importance of human judgment.
- Practice & next steps: Bring it all together with a small, real-world experiment.

## Key learning outcomes

By the end of the course, learners can:

1. Explain AI in everyday language and tell it apart from ordinary automation.
2. Describe how examples shape a model and why its predictions can be wrong.
3. Write a clear prompt, refine a draft, and check the result against a source.
4. Identify a useful, low-risk AI opportunity and define a measure of success.
5. Apply a practical checklist for privacy, fairness, accuracy, and transparency.
6. Plan, test, and explain a small AI-assisted project with human review.

## Module breakdown and activities

### 01. What is artificial intelligence?

**Time:** 45 minutes. **Theme:** AI fundamentals.

The big ideas behind AI, without the technical jargon. Explain AI in everyday language and tell it apart from ordinary automation.

**Key topics:** Start with a familiar example; A rule is not the same as a prediction; A simple map of the terminology.

**Case study: Two ways to sort an inbox.** A rule moves every message from your book club into a folder. A learned filter notices that a new message resembles past spam. The rule is predictable but narrow; the filter can recognize unfamiliar examples, but may also make mistakes. Checking the junk folder is a simple example of human oversight.

**Field exercise: Go on an everyday AI safari.** No account or special software needed. Explore the devices and services you already use.

1. List five digital tasks from your day, such as setting an alarm, searching for a photo, or receiving a recommendation.
2. For each one, decide whether it follows a fixed rule, predicts from examples, or might do both. Mark uncertain examples as "not enough information."
3. Choose one example and explain it to a friend in two sentences. Include one way it could get things wrong.

**Reflection:** Which everyday task uses AI, and what makes it different from a fixed rule?

**Knowledge check:** Which example most clearly uses machine learning?

**Expected reasoning:** The spam filter learns patterns from examples. A timer and basic calculator follow rules written explicitly by people.

### 02. How machines learn

**Time:** 60 minutes. **Theme:** Data & learning.

Explore the relationship between data, patterns, and predictions. Describe how examples shape a model and why its predictions can be wrong.

**Key topics:** Examples are the starting point; Training and using are different; Test on something genuinely new.

**Case study: The fruit sorter and the green apple.** A shop trains a fruit sorter using only red apples and yellow bananas. The sorter appears accurate until a green apple arrives. It may have learned color rather than shape. Adding varied examples and testing on a separate basket gives the shop a better picture of the system's limits.

**Field exercise: Be the model.** Use paper to explore how a small set of examples can teach the wrong lesson.

1. Write down three red apples labeled "apple" and three yellow bananas labeled "banana." Make a rule that predicts the label using color alone.
2. Test your rule on a green apple, a red banana, and a yellow apple. Record which predictions fail.
3. Redesign your examples to include different colors, shapes, and conditions. Reserve three new examples to test your improved rule.

**Reflection:** What was missing from your first set of examples, and how did that affect the result?

**Knowledge check:** What is the best way to check whether a fruit sorter works well?

**Expected reasoning:** New, representative examples test whether the model can apply its patterns beyond the training set.

### 03. Getting to know generative AI

**Time:** 60 minutes. **Theme:** Tools & prompting.

Meet creative AI tools and learn the art of a useful prompt. Write a clear prompt, refine a draft, and check the result against a source.

**Key topics:** From predicting to creating; Give the task a useful shape; Treat the answer as a first draft.

**Case study: A community garden invitation.** Source notes: "Garden open day. Saturday, 10 a.m. Bring water. Entry is free." A draft adds "free lunch included." It sounds welcoming, but lunch was never promised. A good review keeps the useful wording and removes the unsupported detail.

**Field exercise: Write, compare, refine.** Use the garden notes above with a chat assistant, or complete the same exercise on paper. Never use private information.

1. Draft an instruction that names the audience, task, format, and the facts the answer must use.
2. Compare this sample output with the source: "Join us Saturday at 10 a.m. for a free garden visit and lunch. Bring water!" Mark every supported and unsupported claim.
3. Revise the invitation and your prompt. Compare the two drafts for accuracy, clarity, and tone.

**Reflection:** Save your improved prompt and one factual check you would make before sharing the answer.

**Knowledge check:** An AI-written invitation includes a detail missing from your source. What should you do?

**Expected reasoning:** Fluent writing is not evidence. An independent source or the event organizer can verify the detail; otherwise, leave it out.

### 04. AI in everyday work

**Time:** 75 minutes. **Theme:** Real-world applications.

Find practical uses in business, creativity, and daily life. Identify a useful, low-risk AI opportunity and define a measure of success.

**Key topics:** Start with a task, not a tool; Draw a simple workflow; Measure the whole job.

**Case study: A small shop tests reply drafting.** A fictional shop normally spends 12 minutes writing a reply. Its AI draft takes 2 minutes, but fact-checking takes 8 more. The real saving is 2 minutes, not 10. The team tests several ordinary inquiries and reviews accuracy before deciding whether to continue.

**Field exercise: Map one useful opportunity.** Choose a low-stakes task from home, study, or work. A public event announcement is a good first example.

1. Describe the task today, who does it, and what a good result looks like. Estimate a baseline time or quality score.
2. Draw four steps: prepare safe inputs, create a draft, review it, and use the approved result. Name the human reviewer.
3. Compare two approaches on three fictional examples. Record total time and factual errors, then decide whether the tool helped.

**Reflection:** What would your pilot improve, and what evidence would make you stop using it?

**Knowledge check:** Which measure gives the fairest picture of a drafting assistant's value?

**Expected reasoning:** A useful comparison includes the full workflow, including checking and revision, and the quality of the finished result.

### 05. Using AI responsibly

**Time:** 60 minutes. **Theme:** Risks & ethics.

Navigate bias, privacy, and the importance of human judgment. Apply a practical checklist for privacy, fairness, accuracy, and transparency.

**Key topics:** Pause before you paste; Ask who might be left out; Make responsibility visible.

**Case study: The uneven customer survey.** A team summarizes feedback gathered only through its smartphone app. The summary says customers find the app easy to use, but it misses people who could not use it at all. The remedy is not just a better prompt: collect accessible feedback through other channels and note the limitation.

**Field exercise: Run a four-question review.** A fictional club wants to paste its full membership spreadsheet into a public chatbot to plan activities.

1. Privacy: identify personal information that is unnecessary. Replace the spreadsheet with a small fictional set of interests.
2. Fairness and accuracy: list one group that may be missing and one claim that needs independent checking.
3. Accountability: choose a reviewer, describe how members can correct errors, and write one sentence explaining the use of AI.

**Reflection:** Write your own before-I-share checklist: one check each for privacy, fairness, accuracy, and transparency.

**Knowledge check:** What is the safest starting point for a beginner practice exercise?

**Expected reasoning:** Fictional examples avoid exposing real people's information. Removing a name alone may not prevent identification.

### 06. Your first AI project

**Time:** 60 minutes. **Theme:** Practice & next steps.

Bring it all together with a small, real-world experiment. Plan, test, and explain a small AI-assisted project with human review.

**Key topics:** Build a one-page project brief; Try it, check it, document it; Know what to explore next.

**Case study: A clear, checkable event notice.** A learner turns public event notes into a plain-language notice. They compare a handwritten version with an assisted draft, remove an invented detail, and ask a friend to check the result. Their project is not just the final notice: it is the documented process for deciding whether the tool helped.

**Field exercise: Make your first project brief.** Create a one-page record you can share with a friend, colleague, or future you.

1. Set a goal, audience, safe input, and success measure. For a no-account option, reuse the garden notes and sample draft from Module 03.
2. Make a baseline, compare it with an assisted draft, and document at least two checks and one revision.
3. Score accuracy, usefulness, and effort from 1 to 3 for both versions. Write a go, revise, or stop decision, with one next step for the coming week.

**Reflection:** Save your project brief: goal, safe input, approach, comparison, human review, and next step.

**Knowledge check:** What makes a beginner AI pilot ready for a small trial?

**Expected reasoning:** A manageable scope and clear checks let you learn safely. A pilot should make it easy to discover limitations and stop if needed.

## AI tools, frameworks, and trends

- [ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com/) (AI tools): A conversational assistant for drafting, brainstorming, and explaining ideas. Try a simple prompt with fictional information, then check the answer.
- [Teachable Machine](https://teachablemachine.withgoogle.com/) (AI tools): See learning from examples in action. Experiment with an image, sound, or pose classifier in your browser, without writing code.
- [Adobe Firefly](https://firefly.adobe.com/) (AI tools): Explore how written descriptions can create and edit images. Compare prompts and check usage terms before publishing your work.
- [Gemini](https://gemini.google.com/) (AI tools): Explore a multimodal assistant that can work with text and images. Try describing a public-domain picture and compare its answer with what you see.
- [PyTorch](https://pytorch.org/) (Frameworks): A toolkit developers use to build and train learning models. Recognize its role behind the scenes; learning to code is not part of this course.
- [TensorFlow](https://www.tensorflow.org/) (Frameworks): A framework for creating and deploying machine learning models. Explore the project overview to connect the vocabulary with real technology.

**Trends to recognize:** Multimodal systems combine media such as text and images. On-device AI performs some processing locally. Agents can use tools to take several steps toward a goal. Ask what data is shared, which permissions are needed, and where a person reviews the result.

## Final project and assessment

Create a one-page project brief with a goal, an audience, a safe input, a baseline, an assisted draft, quality checks, and a decision to continue, revise, or stop.

The six knowledge checks provide immediate feedback and allow retries. Completing a module records progress in the learner's browser. The capstone is a self-assessed exercise, not an externally graded credential.

## Next steps and additional resources

1. Run one low-risk pilot for a week. Measure the final quality and total effort, including human review.
2. Ask a friend or colleague to check the result. Document a failure case and one improvement.
3. Keep the project brief and a revised example in a small portfolio, excluding private information.
4. Choose a next course or completion certificate that matches your goals. Check current fees and requirements before enrolling.

- [Elements of AI](https://www.elementsofai.com/): Go deeper with an approachable introduction to AI, reasoning, and machine learning. A good next course for curious non-programmers.
- [Google AI Essentials](https://grow.google/ai-essentials/): Continue with workplace exercises and an optional paid course leading to a Google completion certificate. Check current enrollment terms and pricing.
- [People + AI Guidebook](https://pair.withgoogle.com/guidebook/): Practical guidance for putting people first in AI products. Use its questions to plan a small pilot, set expectations, and design human review.
